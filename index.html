Astera Neuro is trying to connect large-scale recordings, anatomical wiring and dynamical models closely enough that a model can suggest the next experiment. The difficult part is deciding whether a neuron is interesting because its activity changes with a state, because it is a structural hub, or because both pieces of evidence agree.

I built a small cross-dataset analysis that joins whole-brain calcium activity from five C. elegans with a 279-neuron connectome, then turns a chosen behavioral contrast into a sensitivity-tested perturbation shortlist. A second analysis runs Astera’s public Clone-Structured Cognitive Graph code on the measured state sequences and checks its predictive value on held-out animals.

How it works

The Kato et al. dataset contributes 15,662 calcium-imaging frames across five unstimulated animals. I z-scored each neuron within recording, retained identified neurons observed in at least two animals, and measured how activity shifts between annotated motor states. Those effects are kept separate from weighted anatomical strength in the Varshney connectome, so the balance can be changed without hiding the tradeoff.

Analysis 1 — Choose a perturbation that survives two kinds of evidence

Method
State contrast × cross-animal agreement × anatomical strength

Choose a motor-state contrast and move the evidence slider. The recommendation changes when a strong physiological marker and a highly connected hub are not the same neuron.

Experimental aim

What this lets the lab do: compare an activity-led and anatomy-led first perturbation, then carry one candidate into a calcium-imaging experiment with an explicit behavioral readout.

Analysis 2 — Does hidden context improve state-sequence prediction?

Result
Leave-one-animal-out evaluation of Astera’s CSCG implementation

A single observed motor label can occur in different temporal contexts. Clone-Structured Cognitive Graphs assign more than one hidden state to the same observation; the question is whether that extra structure predicts a held-out animal better.

The three-clone model reached 0.2567 held-out bits per step versus 0.2634 with one clone, a 2.54% improvement. The fold-to-fold spread is larger than the mean gain, so this is evidence to keep latent context in the model comparison, not evidence that three clones is the final biological answer.

Why this matters for the lab

DynamoBrain’s intern role sits exactly at this junction: process neurophysiology, inspect connectomics, build models of network dynamics and use the result to generate hypotheses with experimental colleagues. The shortlist makes the disagreement between activity and anatomy visible; the held-out model check prevents extra dynamical complexity from being rewarded merely because it fits the training sequence.

Methods note

This is a processed-data analysis, not a reanalysis of raw image volumes. Kato neuron identities are incomplete and differ across animals; only named neurons appearing in at least two recordings and present in the 279-neuron connectome enter the shortlist. Activity is observational and calcium dynamics are slow. The connectome does not encode synaptic sign, state dependence or animal-specific wiring. Full transformations, hashes and limitations are in the provenance record. Sources: Kato et al. 2015, OSF 2395t, Varshney et al. 2011, and the Astera CSCG repository.
